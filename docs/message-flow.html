What actually happens

  1. You speak. The Echo does what it always does — hears the wake word and records.
  2. The audio goes to Amazon. Exactly as it does for a timer or a weather forecast. Amazon's speech service decides which of three intents you meant: on, off, or status.
  3. Alexa calls a small function. It passes a name — PowerOffIntent — and nothing else. No audio and no recording ever reaches my code or your house.
  4. The function picks a command. It looks the intent up in a fixed list of three and publishes the result as a bare name: power_off. It does not build a CAT string, and it cannot send one.
  5. The message reaches your bridge. The bridge holds an outbound connection to AWS that it opened itself, the same way your phone stays connected for notifications. AWS pushes the command down that existing link. No port is forwarded and nothing at your house listens for inbound connections.
  6. The bridge talks to the radio. Only here does CAT exist. The bridge opens a TCP socket to the K4 on port 9200, on your own LAN, and sends PS0;.

“Isn't the Echo just talking to the radio?”

No — and this surprises people, because the Echo and the radio are often sitting on the same network a few feet apart. An Echo cannot run your code. A custom Alexa skill executes in Amazon's cloud, never on the device, so there is no way to make the Echo open a socket to anything. The Echo is a microphone and a speaker; it has no idea the radio exists.

So a command travels out of your house to AWS and back in again, even though the two devices are in the same room. That sounds wasteful, but it buys the thing that matters: the return trip arrives on a connection your bridge dialled out, so your router needs no port forwarding, no dynamic DNS, and no inbound rule of any kind.

Why send a name instead of a CAT string? Because then the cloud half cannot say anything the radio wasn't already going to accept. Even if someone got full control of the function, the bridge only recognises three names and refuses everything else. Arbitrary CAT never crosses the internet.

What if ten friends want this for their own K4s?

Every operator needs their own bridge, on their own network, talking to their own radio. That part can't be shared — it's the half that has to be physically near the transceiver, and it's a small program on a Raspberry Pi that's already doing other things in most shacks.

The cloud half can go either way. The simple version is that each person builds their own: their own skill, their own function, their own credentials, and nothing of theirs touches anything of mine. The alternative is one shared skill that routes each request to the right person's bridge, which is less setup for them and more responsibility for whoever runs it — the routing table becomes the only thing keeping one operator's voice from reaching another operator's transmitter.
